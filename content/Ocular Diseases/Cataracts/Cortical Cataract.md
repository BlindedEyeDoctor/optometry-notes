# Description
**Spoke‑like, radial opacities** in the lens cortex. Often starts inferiorly and progresses circumferentially. 
![[Image - Cortical Cataract.png]]
# TLDR
- Peripheral Vision
- Lens Cortex 
- Spoke-like Appearance
- Shearing of Lens Fibers
- Astigmatic Shift
- Increased Refractive Index
# Linked Symptoms
- Blurred Vision | Partially Blurred Vision
	- Peripheral spokes do not blur vision until higher grades
- Glare|Night Time Glare Sensitivity
	- Dilated pupil evident during night time driving reveals cataract causing greater glare. 
- Diplopia|Monocular Diplopia (Astigmatic Prism)
	- Localized refractive index changes along wedge-like opacities in the pupil zone cause coma-like aberrations and induce astigmatism shifts in refractive error
	- Those that extend into pupillary zone will affect vision
	- May cause monocular doubling
- Contrast Sensitivity Loss|Reduced Contrast Sensitivity
	- Forward light scatter causing increased illumination of the visual scene, decreasing contrast
# Risk Factors
- Age
- Diabetes (risk factor)
- UV Exposure
# Pathophysiology
The pathophysiology of cortical cataracts is also multifactorial:
1. Loss of calcium and sodium homeostasis due to damaged cell membrane epithelial cells that house Na/K ATPase pump
	- Increase in calcium influx, sodium and
	- This leads to overhydration of lens fibres due to osmosis
2. Proteolysis and aggregation of crystallin's occurs and leads to a decrease in soluble proteins and relative increase in insoluble protein
This leads to…
- Ray-like transparent spaces become opaque
- Formation of cuneiform opacities that originate at the periphery of the lens and spread toward the center, leading to an increase in light scatter.
Why does it have a spoke-like appearance?
- Possible explanation is the shearing force between ‘soft’ cortex and ‘hard’ nucleus causing fiber fractures, OR greater UVB radiation on the nasal/inferior lens
